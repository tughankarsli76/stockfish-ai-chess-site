About & Credits

Stockfish AI Chess 1.0
Developed by Tughan Y. Karsli

An independent chess app using open-source engines. Not an official app of the Stockfish project or the other engine authors.

Chess engines

Stockfish 19 and 18 — Stockfish developers; Reckless 0.9.0 — its contributors; PlentyChess 8.0.0 — its contributors; Pawnocchio 2.0.1 — Jonathan Hallström and contributors; Obsidian 16.0 — its contributors. Engine licenses and modified native source are included in the app bundle.

12. Cultural Terminology (Non-Binding Recommendation)
The Şahî (Shahi) Font reflects historical chess and shatranj heritage and is designed with culturally and spiritiually inclusive, religiously neutral principles. Chess pieces have been called with different meanings across languages, eras, and civilizations. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_piece 

When I explain chess to beginners or to people who want a fresh artistic look at the game, I describe the "king" piece as the spiritual leader of the army, and the "queen" piece as the military leader of the army. With these 2 abstract roles, there is room for exploration for the all the different multilingual terminology of chess.

For example, the chess piece known in English as “bishop” is called “elephant” in the majority of the world’s languages, such as Spanish, Chinese, Russian, and Arabic. In old English, this piece was once called an archer. Likewise, the piece called “queen” in modern English chess is associated with an advisor, vizier, or statesman figure in many linguistic traditions, and is also understood as a military leader in languages such as Polish and Hungarian. The French and German word "dame" for the queen piece evolved from the word "firz", which means advisor/vizier. For English-language educational use, the creator’s preferred terminology for the chess pieces is: shah, vizier, rook, elephant, horse, and pawn. These correspond respectively to the king, queen, rook, bishop, knight, and pawn. 

In summary, platforms and educators are welcome and encouraged to use multilingual terminology where appropriate. This recommendation is offered to support cultural context and educational clarity and is not a mandatory naming requirement. Non-English languages are encouraged to continue using their own established chess vocabularies.
